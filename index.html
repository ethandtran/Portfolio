UCI Solar Car

UCI Solar Car is a student-run senior design team that designs, builds, and races a solar-powered vehicle over a two-year cycle to compete in the Formula Sun Grand Prix and the American Solar Challenge. I joined the team in Spring 2025, during which they were in the late-stage manufacturing stages. In preparation for FSGP 2025, my focus was streamlining assembly and integration of the suspension system under tight deadlines.

roleSuspension Engineer team~20 MAE, ~20 EE/CSE, 2 PM
timelineApr 2025 – Jun 2026 toolsSolidWorks, 3D Printing, Laser Cutting

01 · Build

Attaching the steering column

The steering column had to be welded to the chassis in a fixed position, and we had no jig to hold it there. I designed one, starting with a wooden assembly that fit together like puzzle pieces inside the chassis frame. Through iteration I found that repurposing material we already had on hand was the most effective approach, cutting both material waste and production time. The column went on cleanly, and the rest of the team stayed focused on integrating other subsystems.

02 · Problem

Reflecting on FSGP 2025

At the 2025 Formula Sun Grand Prix, last-minute issues with other vehicle systems kept the car off the track for most of the event. The team worked through them, cleared every screening station, and got the car running the dynamic courses on the final day. That was the first real look at how the suspension held up under race conditions. The car's turning radius was too wide to take the figure-eight cleanly, and that became the starting point for the following quarter.

03 · Process

Two fixes over the following quarter

Tracing a wide turning radius to rotor interference

Working back from the wide turning radius, I traced it to interference between the rotor and the control arm during turns. After verifying the control arm's structural integrity, I shaved it down to open up rotor clearance, increasing the steering angle from 12° to 17°.

Replacing washer stacks with printed spacers

The control arms were held between their mounting tabs by stacks of washers, an improvised fix that made every reassembly slightly different and let the suspension geometry drift. I measured the car and prototyped 3D-printed spacers to replace them, giving them a conical geometry so the rod-end bearings kept a wider range of motion. After iterating across multiple sizes, I produced engineering drawings for a final set that preserved the original geometry while making assembly repeatable.

04 · Results

FSGP 2026

During my time with the team, I helped ensure that previously designed parts were actually buildable. Instead of optimizing for what looked clean in CAD, I prioritized what the team could actually fabricate and reassemble under deadline. That shift drove multiple measurable changes: 80% less material on the welding jig, 75% off control arm assembly time across 20+ printed prototypes, and a 25% increase in steering angle from root cause analysis on the suspension system. In 2025, we barely cleared technical inspection on the final day. Fast forward a year later in 2026, and we were the first team to complete it.

SharkNinja

SharkNinja is an American product design and technology company that makes household appliances and cleaning tools. When I started my Co-op there, they were in the final stages of launching the Shark Transformer, which is the first 3-in-1 corded vacuum with an auto-empty system. I joined the Shark Corded NPD Team for six months, working across prototyping, failure analysis, and design for products at every stage between concept and mass production. Alongside that, I took on project management across the product development lifecycle for several SKUs, most notably as engineering lead on the Shark Navigator.

roleMechanical Engineering Co-op core team4 engineers (US), 1 ID, 2 PMs, 1 TPM
timelineJan – Jun 2026 toolsSolidWorks, Creo, Arduino, 3D Printing

01 · Problem

Short of spec, and short of proof

The auto-empty system was the Transformer's headline feature, and it was falling short of performance metrics set by product managers, which were relative to our cordless SKUs already on the market.

However, an even bigger problem sat underneath that one. Test results were inconsistent from run to run, so there was no dependable way to tell whether a given prototype was actually better or whether the number had just moved. Until that was fixed, every design change was a guess.

02 · Process

Three decisions, one feature

Decision 1: Fix the test before touching the design. Results were inconsistent enough that nobody could separate a real improvement from run-to-run variation, and small differences in how a test is set up move the numbers a lot. I wrote documentation defining exactly how each test should be conducted, then worked with the engineers in China running them to quality control their results against it. That gave the team one fixed basis to compare against, so everything after this point could be judged on data instead of noise.

Decision 2: Solve it inside the dust cup. By the time I picked the problem up, most of the vacuum was already locked, and reworking anything upstream would have meant new tooling the program couldn't justify. So I confined the solution to the one area still open. Across 15+ prototypes in SolidWorks and Creo I learned how debris actually moved inside the hand vacuum's dust cup during an auto-empty cycle, and landed on a fin geometry that redirected it toward the evacuation path: fast to implement, no tooling impact elsewhere, and the best auto-empty performance of anything tested.

Decision 3: Treat manufacturing as part of the design. A geometry that works on a prototype only counts if it survives production. We built overmolding into the fin to absorb small tolerance variations between parts. Those gaps matter more than they sound: fine debris like hair catches in them and accumulates, and once that happens the auto-empty system stops evacuating properly. Working it out with the manufacturing team meant catching problems like that while they were still cheap to fix, rather than after the product reached the market.

03 · Solution

Proving the fix held up

The fin exceeded the debris capacity KPI by 25% and cleared the auto-empty system for production launch. The testing protocol outlasted the project, giving the team a repeatable basis for comparing results on everything that came after it.

However, clearing the auto-empty KPI wasn't enough on its own. To ensure that the fin didn't introduce any trade-offs, I ran tests to measure airwatts, which are a unit of measurement for vacuum suction and cleaning efficiency. I then compared units with and without the fin installed, and the results landed within margin of each other, confirming the fin fixed the auto-empty system without sacrificing suction performance.

04 · Failure Analysis

Tracing a failure to its source

The same approach closed out root cause action items on a legacy SKU. On the Shark Rocket Turbo, an articulating squeegee failure surfaced through DFMEA was severely degrading reverse cleaning. I executed testing protocols targeting motor suction power and nozzle geometry, coordinated the fixes across engineering, testing, and product teams, and improved debris pickup efficiency by 90%.

05 · Personal Project

Addressing the #1 customer complaint

The most common complaint customers have about vacuums is that suction fades over time. The cause usually isn't the motor, it's filters that were never cleaned. The user manual says to clean them regularly, but most people never read the manual, so the instruction never reaches the person who needs it.

On my own initiative, I built an Arduino-based differential pressure system that monitors filter health and tells the user on the vacuum itself, in real time, when it needs cleaning. I presented the proof of concept to leadership, who approved it for further development across future SKUs.

06 · Reflection

What shipped

During my co-op, most of my work lived in the gap between what a product does on a spec sheet and how it performs in someone's home. Instead of treating a passing test as a finished feature, I prioritized what the customer would actually experience, and made sure we could measure it consistently before calling anything solved. That drove multiple measurable changes: 25% over the debris capacity KPI on the auto-empty system, a 90% improvement in debris pickup on a legacy SKU, and a filter health concept approved for future development. The most valuable thing I learned was adopting a consumer-oriented mindset and fixing any product problems before they arise.

UCI Keyak Lab

Dr. Joyce Keyak's biomedical engineering lab at UC Irvine is exploring phosphorus-32 (P-32) beta emission therapy as an alternative to conventional x-ray radiation for treating cancerous tumors in the vertebrae. Because the safe radiation dose for spinal neurons is still unknown, the lab needed a way to deliver precise, repeatable doses of P-32 to cultured neuron cells for study. I joined as an undergraduate researcher to help design the irradiation chamber that makes that possible, working across CAD design and fabrication to build its shielding and alignment fixtures. More about her research.

roleUndergraduate Researcher team4 undergrad, 1 PI
timelineFeb 2025 – Jun 2026 toolsSolidWorks, Power Tools, Bandsaw, Drill Press

01 · Constraints

Three constraints, one piece

When I joined the lab, the team already had a preliminary design for the irradiation chamber. My task was to design the shielding pieces: parts that would minimize radiation exposure to personnel while also securing consistent placement of the microelectrode array (MEA), the surface the neuron cells are cultured on. I took measurements directly from the existing CAD model so the new pieces would fit inside the chamber, while satisfying three constraints: holding a 90 mm petri dish at a sloped position, securing a 49 x 49 mm MEA chip within the dish at a fixed position and rotation, and shielding the steel screws from P-32 beta emissions with at least 1/4" to 1/2" of plastic.

02 · Process

Redesigning for a better fit

While assembling the chamber and shield pieces in SolidWorks, I found that the square shape formed by the shield pieces didn't fit inside the round petri dish. I couldn't just make the pieces thinner either, since that would have compensated the amount of shielding they provided. So I redesigned the pieces to cut the corners at an angle, keeping enough plastic over the P-32 vial while letting the assembly fit inside the dish. After some fine-tuning, the design was ready for fabrication.

03 · Fabrication

Accounting for tolerances

I used a bandsaw to cut sheets of polycarbonate to size, then a drill press to add holes for the screws, which I tapped by hand so they could thread into the chamber. The angled corner cuts were the hardest part to get right on the bandsaw, so after some early struggles we switched to powered cutting tools, which were faster and safer. Even then, the shield pieces fit inconsistently inside the petri dish, since how tightly they were screwed into the chamber shifted their positioning slightly. I filed down the edges of each piece until they fit securely no matter their orientation.

04 · Reflection

Building without a blueprint

This project is still in progress, but it taught me a lot about working inside a research environment. Unlike a defined product spec, there was no predetermined path to reach the lab's goal, so a lot of the process was experimentation: design a piece, test whether it held up, and adjust based on what I learned. The final design for the irradiation chamber isn't locked in yet, but each iteration has narrowed down what actually works. Here's the first version of the shield pieces fitting inside the chamber and petri dish. That habit of testing small and iterating is what introduced me to the iterative design process and attracted me to product environments.

Autonomous Rover

For ENGR 7B at UC Irvine, my team designed and built an autonomous rover that could follow a predetermined path and pick up a detected object, inside a $300 budget and a 12" x 16" footprint, with an accessible battery switch and no sharp corners. We based the design on Gleep Glorp, a viral internet meme of an edited photo of a green alien cat, and I led the CAD design and manufacturing.

roleLead Mechanical Engineer team7 students
timelineJan – Mar 2025 toolsSolidWorks, 3D Printing, Laser Cutting, Arduino

01 · Design

Designing for assembly

I had no prior SolidWorks experience going into this project, so designing the chassis and a functional claw mechanism meant learning the software as I went. Partway through, I added a second layer to the chassis to improve wire management and weight distribution, which also let me repurpose the front and back walls to mount the rover's three IR sensors, one in front and two in back, angled vertically to the floor for better line detection. Most of the claw components came off the 3D printer, while the chassis itself was laser cut.

02 · Troubleshooting

Keeping the rover on track

We overlooked how weight distribution would affect ground contact between the front caster and rear wheels, which made the rover turn better right than left despite a centered battery. That imbalance also tilted the chassis just enough to change the angle between the IR sensors and the floor, throwing off how they read the track and sending the rover off course entirely. The fix was almost comically simple: taping a wrench to the back balanced the rover enough for every wheel to make even contact, and it completed the track from there. Looking back, leaving more room in the CAD for adjustments, especially around the wiring and IR sensors, would have made tweaks like this much easier to make mid-build instead of after the fact.

03 · Reflection

Learning by doing

Volunteering to lead the CAD design and manufacturing pushed me out of my comfort zone, since I hadn't touched SolidWorks before this project. Outside of my main role, I also helped my teammates debug the Arduino code, which gave me some early exposure to C++ and basic wiring. Between the design work and the troubleshooting along the way, this project was my first real introduction to design for manufacturing and assembly, principles I'd lean on again in every project after it.

RC Drone

Through the Peterworks program with ASME at UCI, I worked with a team of four students to build a drone capable of controlled flight inside a cage. The requirements were narrow: the electronics had to be fully enclosed, and the drone needed landing legs and blade guards to protect the propellers on touchdown. Past that, the design was wide open, so my group modeled ours after the battle bus from Fortnite.

roleLead Design Engineer team4 students
timelineJan – Jun 2025 toolsSolidWorks, 3D Printing, Laser Cutting

01 · Design

Modeling a battle bus that flies

After taking measurements of the electronics we'd be building around, we split up the design work, and I took the chassis: seven components in total, including the side panels with windows, the front and back ends, the top and bottom, and the front windshield. I built in bolt-and-nut mounting holes throughout, and the chassis and landing legs came off the 3D printer while the arms were laser cut. Hole shrinkage during printing and a few laser cutter failures meant hand-drilling some holes after the fact, and we ended up switching the landing legs from PLA to TPU partway through for durability.

02 · Testing

No room left inside

A lot of flaws only became obvious once we hit assembly and testing. We'd designed the drone too small for the electronics it needed to hold, which meant cutting into the window panels just to fit the ESCs and cramming the rest of the wiring in wherever it would go. The tight hole placement that looked fine in CAD made assembly genuinely difficult in a space that small.

03 · Reflection

No takeoff, but plenty of takeaways

While the project wasn't entirely a success, I still gained valuable experience and some key takeaways. Out of 15 groups in the program, only 1 drone took flight successfully, so we believe that this might be partly due to weak motors that have been reused throughout multiple years. Still, there are other variables we could've controlled, applying DFMA principles for our drone chassis to make repair of the drone smoother. Planning for the future during the engineering design process was emphasized during this project.

RC Rover

ENGR 7A at UCI was my first real exposure to engineering as a discipline. Alongside a team of seven students, we designed and manufactured a remote-controlled rover to navigate an obstacle course, inside a $300 budget and a 10" x 16" size constraint, with a removable battery and an accessible on/off switch. My role centered on component research and fabrication.

roleR&D Engineer team6 students
timelineSept – Dec 2024 toolsSolidWorks, Soldering, Band Saw, Drill Press

01 · Process

Picking parts before touching tools

Most of my time went into researching and sourcing components before we ever touched fabrication. We settled on an Italian poplar plywood chassis for its balance of weight and durability, 85 mm wheels, the largest available option for handling the obstacle course terrain, and a 52-tooth hub-mount gear to balance torque against speed. I cut and drilled the chassis on the band saw and drill press while our electronics teammates handled the soldering.

02 · Testing

What held up, and what didn't

We tested in stages: individual components first, then sub-assemblies, then the full rover. The parallel steering mechanism we landed on achieved a 57-degree steering angle, which held up through most of testing. Where it didn't hold up was the official run itself, where the chassis took damage falling off a ramp, the rover's length caused it to jam on an incline, and the 3D-printed steering mechanism needed reprinting more than once due to shrinkage. We also ran into motor mounting misalignment that only showed up once everything was assembled together.

03 · Reflection

What CAD doesn't show you

As my first engineering project, this one taught me that a clean CAD model doesn't always translate perfectly into a real part, whether that's shrinkage in a 3D print or a misalignment that only shows up once everything is bolted together. If I built this again, I'd prototype earlier and weigh manufacturability more heavily during the design phase, rather than treating it as something to solve once the parts were already in hand.

courseMAE 52 time~50 hours
toolsSolidWorks, SolidWorks Visualize, Finite Element Analysis

For my MAE 52 class at UC Irvine, I decided to model Zekrom, a legendary Pokémon in SolidWorks. At this point, I already had previous experience with SolidWorks, so I wanted to challenge myself by exploring new SolidWorks features, such as revolves, surfaces, and split lines. I also wanted Zekrom's joints to move properly, which included implementing assembly mates that would allow his tail and head to spin, wings to flap, and legs, arms, and fingers to function properly. I then utilized SolidWorks Visualize to create a render of Zekrom, as depicted below. I also conducted finite element analysis to analyze how strong Zekrom's tail would be during a hypothetical battle.
